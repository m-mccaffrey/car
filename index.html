EV Conversion Study · 1976–1987 Chevrolet Chevette

Three ways to make a Chevette electric.

The Chevette is a featherweight, rear-wheel-drive, body-on-a-budget subcompact — which makes it a near-ideal EV-swap donor. This study designs three complete conversions, selects every major subsystem component, and compares them head-to-head on cost, range, acceleration and top speed. Two of the three are single-speed direct-drive builds.

The Platform

Why the Chevette is a great EV donor

Three traits matter for a conversion: low mass, a simple rear-drive layout, and a roomy engine bay. The Chevette has all three, plus a body-on-frame-style subframe that takes battery boxes well.

Light & simple

At roughly 2,150 lb stock, the Chevette weighs less than a modern compact. Pulling the 1.6 L iron four, gearbox-optional layout, fuel tank, exhaust and radiator removes ~350 lb before any battery goes in.

Rear-wheel drive

A longitudinal engine driving a solid rear axle is the easiest layout to electrify: a motor can be coupled to the original gearbox, bolted to the differential, or replaced wholesale by an integrated drive unit.

Honest geometry

A long-ish 94.3 in wheelbase, flat floors and a deep transmission tunnel give clean places to mount packs low and centred, keeping the centre of gravity sane as weight is added.

Design target  Every build below targets a road-legal, daily-drivable car that keeps the Chevette's ~50/50-ish balance, uses commercially available conversion parts, and runs a modern lithium pack with a real BMS.
Component Selection

The major subsystems

An EV conversion is a system of ~12 subsystems. Below is what each one does and the menu of parts the three designs draw from.

◆ Traction motor

Converts electrical to mechanical power. Choices span a NetGain HyPer 9 HV (SRIPM, 90 kW / 162 lb-ft) up to a salvaged Tesla Large Drive Unit (induction, ~310 kW).

◆ Inverter / controller (VCU)

Turns pack DC into 3-phase AC and runs the torque map and regen. HyPer-Drive X144 for the HyPer 9; an AEM EV VCU + LDU control board for the Tesla unit.

◆ Drivetrain coupling

Either a direct-drive single ratio (motor → differential, or an integrated drive unit) or the retained 4-speed manual via an adapter plate for gear multiplication.

◆ Battery pack

Energy storage. LiFePO4 prismatic cells for safety/longevity on a budget, or higher-density Tesla NMC modules for range and power.

◆ Battery management (BMS)

Cell balancing, temperature and over/under-voltage protection. A distributed Orion / JK / Tesla-native BMS depending on chemistry.

◆ On-board charger

Rectifies AC mains to charge the pack. 3.3 kW (Level 1/2) up to an 11 kW unit for the big pack, behind a J1772 inlet.

◆ DC-DC converter

Steps the HV pack down to 12–14 V to run lights, wipers and accessories in place of the alternator.

◆ Cabin heat & A/C

A PTC ceramic heater replaces the coolant heater core; an optional electric A/C compressor on the larger build.

◆ Brakes & vacuum

An electric vacuum pump feeds the booster; the controllers add regenerative braking to recover energy.

◆ HV safety

Main contactors, a HV fuse, manual service disconnect (MSD), inertia switch and shielded orange cabling — non-negotiable on every build.

Why direct drive here

The HyPer 9 makes full torque from zero rpm, so the gearbox is optional. Deleting it and coupling the motor straight to the original differential saves weight, cost and a failure point. The trade-off is no gear multiplication — launch and hill-climb suffer, and top speed is capped by motor rpm against the fixed final drive.

Why keep the gearbox

Bolting the same motor to the stock 4-speed via an adapter plate multiplies torque in the low gears. The result is markedly quicker acceleration and confident grades from the same motor — at the cost of added mass, an adapter plate, and the need to shift (or clutchless up-shift).

Why a Tesla unit

A salvaged Tesla Large Drive Unit is a self-contained motor, single-speed reduction and differential — the EV equivalent of an LS swap. It is inherently direct drive. It demands the most fabrication (the solid axle is replaced and an aftermarket VCU added) but transforms the car.

The Verdict

Which one should you build?

Pick Commuter if…

…you want the cheapest, simplest, lightest path to a clean, reliable around-town EV, your trips are under ~30 miles each way, and you value efficiency and low fabrication over straight-line speed.

Pick Sport if…

…you want the best all-round driver — genuinely quick, freeway-capable, ~100-mile range — and you enjoy rowing a gearbox. It is the sweet spot for pace-and-range per dollar.

Pick GT if…

…you want a giant-killer: sub-4.5 s to 60, 200+ mile range, fast charging, and you have the budget, skills and tools for serious rear-end and HV fabrication.

How These Numbers Were Made

Methodology & assumptions

Performance modelling

Acceleration is estimated from motor torque, drivetrain ratio and modelled curb weight using a simple tractive-force model with a ~0.85 traction/driveline efficiency factor; top speed from peak motor rpm against the final-drive ratio and rolling/aero drag. Range uses modelled efficiency (Wh/mi) against usable pack energy at ~90% depth of discharge. Figures are rounded and intended for comparison, not as guarantees.

Cost & parts basis

Component prices reflect mid-2026 US conversion-market pricing from vendors such as EV West, ampREVOLT and NetGain, with salvage ranges for Tesla hardware. Costs are parts-only and exclude the donor vehicle, registration, and labour. A direct-drive build is defined here as a single fixed reduction with no shiftable gearbox.

Safety  High-voltage EV work is hazardous. A compliant build needs proper HV interlocks, fusing, isolation monitoring and, in many jurisdictions, an inspection. Treat this study as a design comparison, not assembly instructions.
